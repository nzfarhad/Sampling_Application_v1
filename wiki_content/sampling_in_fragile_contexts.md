# Sampling in Fragile, Humanitarian and Development Contexts

## Purpose
This page provides practical guidance for Monitoring, Evaluation and Research (M&E/R) teams working in fragile, conflict-affected, or humanitarian settings where access, population lists and security constraints limit standard probability sampling.

## Key constraints in fragile settings
- Access restrictions and safety risks for enumerators and respondents
- Displaced, mobile or partially enumerated populations
- Incomplete, outdated or non-existent sampling frames
- Limited time and resources; need for rapid assessments
- Political sensitivity and ethical concerns

## Practical principles
1. Prioritise safety and do no harm: stop or adapt data collection where safety is at risk.
2. Document assumptions and limitations clearly (frame quality, access bias, non-response).
3. Use mixed evidence: combine remote data, administrative records, community lists, and key informant inputs.
4. Prefer sample designs that are implementable in the field given your constraints; transparency is better than false precision.

## Methods and when to use them
- Cluster sampling (with PPS): Good when you lack a full list but can sample clusters (villages, camps) and within-cluster listing is feasible. Common in household surveys in humanitarian settings.

- Multi-stage sampling with local listing: Practical when official frames are poor but local teams can list households or structures within selected clusters. Accept increased design effect.

- Lot Quality Assurance Sampling (LQAS): Useful for classification (e.g., areas meeting/not meeting a target). Requires small sample sizes per lot and is rapid and decision-focused.

- Respondent-Driven Sampling (RDS): Suitable for hidden or networked populations (e.g., people who inject drugs, undocumented migrants) where peer recruitment is viable. Requires careful analysis and implementation.

- Time-Location or Venue-Based Sampling: For mobile or public populations (markets, transit points). Useful when people congregate at predictable locations/times.

- Adaptive and network sampling: For rare or clustered phenomena; sample rules change based on observed data.

- Purposive / quota / convenience samples: Used for rapid assessments, qualitative work, or when probability methods are impossible. Always report limitations and avoid over-generalising.

## Practical implementation checklist
1. Rapid context assessment: map areas, access constraints, likely population movements.
2. Identify feasible sampling units: clusters, camps, market locations, mobile teams.
3. Decide on sample size and allocation balancing precision and feasibility.
4. Prepare tools for partial listing or segment selection inside clusters.
5. Use safety protocols and remote data collection where in-person is risky.
6. Train enumerators on consent, sensitivity and referral pathways.
7. Pre-test the approach and document deviations.

## Handling frames and denominators
- If an official denominator (population) is missing, consider:
  - Using satellite imagery or gridded population datasets to estimate denominators
  - Using community lists validated by local leaders
  - Combining administrative data with key informant estimates

## Adjusting analysis and reporting
- Report design effects, intra-cluster correlation (ICC) assumptions, and limitations.
- Present results with appropriate uncertainty and avoid false precision.
- Use sensitivity analysis to show how results change under different assumptions about frame quality or coverage.

## Quick decision matrix (high level)
- Good frame + safe access: Probability sampling (SRS, stratified, cluster with PPS)
- Poor frame + cluster access possible: Multi-stage cluster with local listing
- Need area-level classification/rapid decision: LQAS
- Hidden populations: RDS or network sampling
- Mobile/public populations: Time-location sampling
- No feasible probability option: Purposive/rapid assessment with clear limits
