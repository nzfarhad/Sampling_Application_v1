## Overview

In humanitarian and development settings, selecting the right sampling method is critical for generating reliable, ethical, and actionable data. This guide compares **Probability Sampling** and **Non-Probability Sampling**, highlighting their strengths, limitations, and use cases in fragile contexts.

---
  
  ## Probability Sampling
  
  ### Definition
  A sampling method where every unit in the population has a known, non-zero chance of being selected.

### Key Features
- Enables statistical inference
- Allows estimation of sampling error
- Supports generalization to the broader population

### Common Methods
- **Simple Random Sampling**: Equal chance for all units
- **Systematic Sampling**: Selects every kth unit from a list or ordered population after a random starting point, ensuring a regular interval of selection.
- **Stratified Sampling**: Divides population into subgroups (e.g., age, gender)
- **Cluster Sampling**: Selects entire groups (e.g., villages, blocks)


### Advantages
- High reliability and validity
- Transparent and replicable
- Suitable for large-scale surveys and evaluations

### Limitations in Humanitarian Contexts
- Requires accurate sampling frames
- May be impractical in insecure or inaccessible areas
- Can be resource-intensive

---
  
  ## Non-Probability Sampling
  
  ### Definition
  A sampling method where selection is based on judgment, convenience, or accessibility, and not all units have a known chance of selection.

### Key Features
- Does not support statistical inference
- Cannot estimate sampling error
- Often used when probability sampling is not feasible

### Common Methods
- **Convenience Sampling**: Based on ease of access
- **Purposive Sampling**: Targets specific subgroups (e.g., survivors of GBV)
- **Snowball Sampling**: Used for hidden or stigmatized populations
- **Quota Sampling**: Ensures representation of key characteristics

### Advantages
- Flexible and fast
- Useful in rapid assessments
- Can reach marginalized or mobile populations

### Limitations
- Higher risk of bias
- Limited generalizability
- Requires careful documentation and transparency

---
  
  ## Choosing the Right Method
  
| **Criteria**             | **Probability Sampling**     | **Non-Probability Sampling**    |
|---------------------------|------------------------------|---------------------------------|
| Statistical Inference     | ✅ Yes                       | ❌ No                           |
| Sampling Error Estimation | ✅ Possible                  | ❌ Not possible                 |
| Frame Requirement         | ✅ Required                  | ❌ Not required                 |
| Use in Fragile Contexts   | ⚠️ Limited by feasibility    | ✅ More adaptable               |
| Ethical Considerations    | ✅ Strong protocols          | ✅ Requires careful oversight   |
| Speed and Flexibility     | ❌ Slower                    | ✅ Faster                       |


  ---
  
  ## Best Practices
  
  - Clearly define your population and objectives
- Assess feasibility and access constraints early
- Use mixed methods when probability sampling is not viable
- Document assumptions, limitations, and ethical safeguards
- Pilot your sampling approach before full deployment
