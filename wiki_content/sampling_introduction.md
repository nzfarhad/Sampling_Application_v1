# Introduction to Sampling

## What is Sampling?

Sampling is the process of selecting a subset of individuals or units from a larger population to make inferences about the entire population. In research and data collection, it's often impractical or impossible to study every member of a population, so we use sampling to obtain representative data.

## Why is Sampling Important?

1. **Cost Efficiency**: Studying an entire population can be expensive and time-consuming
2. **Feasibility**: Some populations are too large or inaccessible to study completely
3. **Accuracy**: Well-designed samples can provide accurate estimates of population parameters
4. **Speed**: Sampling allows for faster data collection and analysis

## Key Concepts

### Population
The entire group of individuals or units that you want to study. This could be:
- All households in a city
- All students in a school
- All beneficiaries of a program

### Sample
A subset of the population that is actually studied. The sample should be representative of the population.

### Sampling Frame
A list or database that contains all the units in the population from which the sample will be drawn.

### Sampling Unit
The individual element that is selected for study (e.g., a household, a person, a school).

## Types of Sampling

### Probability Sampling
- Every unit in the population has a known, non-zero chance of being selected
- Allows for statistical inference and estimation of sampling error
- Examples: Simple random sampling, stratified sampling, cluster sampling

### Non-Probability Sampling
- Units are selected based on convenience, judgment, or other non-random criteria
- Cannot estimate sampling error or make statistical inferences
- Examples: Convenience sampling, purposive sampling, snowball sampling

## Sampling Error vs. Non-Sampling Error

### Sampling Error
- Error that occurs because we're studying a sample rather than the entire population
- Can be reduced by increasing sample size
- Can be estimated and controlled through proper sampling design

### Non-Sampling Error
- Error that occurs due to problems in data collection, measurement, or processing
- Examples: measurement error, non-response bias, data entry errors
- Cannot be reduced by increasing sample size

## Best Practices

1. **Define your population clearly**
2. **Create a comprehensive sampling frame**
3. **Choose an appropriate sampling method**
4. **Determine adequate sample size**
5. **Implement quality control measures**
6. **Document your sampling process**

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- **Selection bias**: When the sampling process systematically excludes certain groups
- **Non-response bias**: When selected units don't participate
- **Undercoverage**: When some population units are not included in the sampling frame
- **Overcoverage**: When the sampling frame includes units not in the target population

## Using sampling in fragile and humanitarian contexts

In development, humanitarian and fragile settings, common assumptions (complete frames, free access, stable populations) often do not hold. Below are quick points to consider when planning sampling in these contexts:

- Assess access constraints early: security, permissions, seasonal access, and movement restrictions affect where you can sample.
- Evaluate the availability and quality of sampling frames (census lists, beneficiary lists, administrative records). Often these are incomplete or outdated.
- Prioritise safety and ethics: ensure data collection does not increase risk to participants or enumerators.
- Use mixed-methods approaches where probability sampling is not feasible: combine remote sensing, satellite-derived denominators, purposive or quota samples with careful documentation.
- Choose practical methods such as cluster sampling with probability proportional to size (PPS), Lot Quality Assurance Sampling (LQAS), respondent-driven sampling (RDS) for hidden populations, or adaptive/time-location sampling for mobile groups.

Detailed guidance for fragile contexts is available in the Methods section ("Sampling in Fragile Contexts").
